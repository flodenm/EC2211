## 1. U.S. debt sustainability

The average annual growth rate of nominal GDP in the United States was 4.6 percent between 2000Q1 and 2026Q2. During that time period, the average interest rate on U.S. government bonds with 10 years to maturity was 3.4 percent. According to the IMF, the U.S. government debt is currently 127 percent of GDP (general government gross debt). Assume, for simplicity, that the 10-year bond yield is the interest rate on the entire government debt stock.

- **a.** What level of primary surplus relative to GDP would stabilize the U.S. debt ratio if the historical interest and growth rates also apply for the future?

- **b.** The interest rate on 10-year bonds has risen to 5.1 percent. The IMF forecasts that average nominal GDP growth for the U.S. economy will be 4.1 percent per year in the coming five years and that the U.S. government will run a primary deficit of 3.7 percent per year. With these interest and growth rates:

  - **(i)** Starting with debt-to-GDP at 127 percent of GDP, how high would the debt-to-GDP ratio be one year later if the primary balance remains at $-3.7$ percent of GDP?

  - **(ii)** How much fiscal consolidation is needed to stabilize the debt ratio?

## 2. Debt dynamics when $i<g$

The average annual growth rate of nominal GDP in the United States was 4.1 percent from 2010 to 2019. In the same time period, the average interest rate on 1-year U.S. government bonds was 0.7 percent.

Consider now the following strategy: We issue a bond $B_{2026}$ to finance some immediate government spending. When the bond matures next year, we roll over the debt by issuing a new bond $B_{2027}=(1+i)B_{2026}$.

- **a.** Suppose that the above interest and growth rates persist. How will our rolled over bond $B_t$ then develop over time in relation to GDP?

- **b.** Do you see any problems associated with this strategy?

## 3. Imports and the multiplier

(Based on exercise 9 in Jones chapter 11) The amount of goods that the Swedish economy imports might depend on the current state of the economy as well as on potential GDP. For example, when the economy is booming, imports usually rise. To incorporate this channel into the IS-MP model, suppose that the import equation is given by 

$$
\frac{IM_t}{\bar{Y}_t} = \alpha_{IM} + \eta \tilde{Y}_t.
$$

 Assume that the remainder of the IS-MP model is unchanged from the original setup in LN6.

- **a.** Derive the IS curve for this new specification.

- **b.** What is the economic explanation for why the $\eta$ parameter shows up in the denominator of the new IS curve? Notice that an aggregate demand shock that that increases $\alpha$ by one percentage point now has a *smaller* effect on output than it did in the original IS curve. Why?

- **c.** Do you think that empirically plausible values for $\eta$ are the same for Sweden and the United States? Why or why not?

## 5. Currency crises and the demand for dollars

## 7. Transition economies

Consider an economy that has just integrated with richer and more advanced economies. Suppose that it has a well-educated population but an old and outdated capital stock. People expect income to grow rapidly in the coming decades. (Think for example of Eastern European countries in the 1990s and early 2000s, hoping to join the European Union.)

- **a.** According to the permanent income hypothesis, how should households’ consumption and savings behavior respond to this development?

- **b.** What is likely the marginal product of capital in this economy? How do you think investment will respond to this development?

- **c.** What does all this imply for the current account balance in the country?
