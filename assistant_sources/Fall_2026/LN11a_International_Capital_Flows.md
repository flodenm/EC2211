## The dollar and the international monetary system

### 1. The U.S. dollar is the international reserve currency

[-Miran, p. 6]
    "Because America provides the reserve assets to the world, there is demand for U.S. dollars and U.S. Treasury securities that is not rooted in balancing trade or in optimizing risk-adjusted returns."

### What does an international currency do?

The same currency can perform several international functions:
- **medium of exchange**: vehicle currency in foreign-exchange
              trading and international payments
- **unit of account**: currency used to invoice trade and issue
              international debt
- **store of value**: currency held in private portfolios and
              official reserves
- **anchor**: currency against which other currencies are
              stabilized

    These uses reinforce one another through network effects.

### The foreign-exchange market in 2025

- Average daily turnover was **USD 9.6 trillion** in April 2025
- The dollar was on one side of **89%** of all transactions
- The euro appeared in 29% and the yen in 17%
- The shares sum to 200% because every transaction involves two
              currencies
- The largest trading centers were the United Kingdom, the United
              States, Singapore and Hong Kong SAR

 Source: [BIS 2025 Triennial Central Bank Survey](https://www.bis.org/publications/triennial-central-bank-survey-foreign-exchange-and-over-the-counter-otc-derivatives-markets-2025).

### Dollar dominance extends beyond foreign-exchange trading

- roughly half of international payments
- around 55–60% of international banking claims and liabilities
- around 60% of foreign-currency debt issuance
- about one-third of marketable U.S. Treasury securities held by
                  foreign investors in early 2025

**Figure source:** `Figs/Rb_fxreserves.png`

         The Riksbank's foreign-exchange reserves are mainly held in
        U.S. dollars.

      See: Bertaut, von Beschwitz and Curcuru (2025), [``The International Role of the U.S. Dollar''](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-international-role-of-the-u-s-dollar-2025-edition-20250718.html)

### Why is the U.S. dollar the dominant international currency, and is that
      dominance under threat?

- A large economy with deep and liquid financial
              markets
- A large supply of assets (U.S. government bonds) that are
              widely viewed as safe and easy to trade
- The dollar can be converted freely and U.S. capital markets are open
- Property rights and the rule of law support confidence in dollar assets
- Network effects make an already dominant currency convenient to use

    Switching is costly, but dominance can weaken if the issuer damages the
        liquidity, safety or openness that made its currency attractive.

### Is the dollar's international role under threat?

The dollar remains dominant, but its share of global foreign-exchange
    reserves has gradually declined:
- from about 72% in 2001 to about 57% in early 2026
- reserve managers have diversified mainly into several smaller
              currencies rather than one clear competitor
- the euro accounts for about 20% and the renminbi only about 2%
- central banks have also increased their holdings of gold, which is
              not included in these foreign-exchange-reserve figures

## The Triffin dilemma: Does dollar dominance require persistent U.S. deficits?

### 2. Does dollar dominance require persistent U.S. deficits?

[-Miran, p. 6]
        "In Triffin world, reserve assets are a form of global money supply, and demand for them is a function of global trade and savings, not the domestic trade balance or return characteristics of the reserve nation."

### The original Triffin dilemma

Triffin's argument concerned the Bretton Woods system:
- Foreign central banks held dollar reserves that were convertible
              into U.S. gold at a fixed price
- Growth in international trade and liquidity increased the demand
              for dollar reserve assets
- The stock of dollar claims held abroad consequently grew relative
              to the U.S. gold stock
- This could eventually undermine confidence in the promise to
              convert dollars into gold

    The United States therefore faced a tension between supplying international
    liquidity and maintaining confidence in gold convertibility.

### Modern versions of the Triffin argument

Two related arguments should be distinguished:

- **The safe-asset argument**: growing global demand for safe
              dollar assets requires the United States to supply a growing
              stock of Treasury securities or other dollar liabilities

- **The current-account argument**: demand for dollar assets
              generates capital inflows, an appreciated dollar and persistent
              U.S. current-account deficits

    Miran combines these arguments: dollar dominance allows the United States
    to borrow cheaply, but is also claimed to produce trade deficits and
    pressure on U.S. manufacturing.

### What does balance-of-payments accounting tell us?

From LN11b:

$$FA=CA=S-I$$

    If foreigners acquire additional net claims on the United States ($FA<0$):
- the United States runs a current account deficit ($CA<0$)
- U.S. investment exceeds national saving

    However, these identities do not establish why saving and investment differ.

    Accounting is not causality: Miran's argument requires a mechanism through which demand for
        dollar assets affects interest rates, the exchange rate, saving and investment.

### 3. Does supplying reserve assets require a U.S. deficit?

- Foreign central banks can purchase Treasury securities from
              existing holders rather than finance new U.S. borrowing
- U.S. current-account deficits have persisted since 2015 even
              though foreign official purchases of U.S. assets have been small
- Demand for dollar assets may affect interest rates and the
              exchange rate, but an additional mechanism is needed to explain
              how this changes U.S. saving and investment
Assessment
        Dollar dominance may encourage the United States to issue more debt,
        but it does not mechanically require a current-account deficit.

     See Bordo and McCauley ([2017](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-684-triffin-dilemma-or-myth), [2025](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/miran-were-not-triffin-land-anymore)).

## Benefits and costs of issuing the dominant currency

### 4. The exorbitant privilege

[-Miran, p. 8]
        "Because there is a persistent reserve-driven demand for UST securities, the United States may be able to borrow at lower yields than would otherwise be the case."

### The exorbitant privilege

The exorbitant privilege
        Reserve currency status $\rightarrow$ high demand for U.S. treasury bonds $\rightarrow$ low borrowing costs for the U.S. government

- Term coined in 1960s by French government representatives
- The Bretton Woods system (1944–1971)
  - U.S. dollar pegged to gold
  - All other currencies pegged to USD
- France complained that U.S. fiscal policy was too expansionary
  - ... and that France indirectly sponsored the Vietnam war
- The Bretton Woods system broke down but USD dominance has persisted

### Safe and liquid assets provide a convenience yield

Investors value Treasury securities not only for their financial return but
    also for their safety, liquidity and usefulness as collateral.

    They may therefore accept a lower yield than on otherwise comparable assets.
    This yield difference is referred to as a **convenience yield**.

    Strong international demand for Treasury securities can consequently reduce
    U.S. government borrowing costs.

    This benefit is not guaranteed: concerns about fiscal sustainability,
    market functioning or the treatment of foreign investors can reduce the
    convenience yield. { Recent evidence: [The Economist: ``The rich world is flirting with fiscal disaster''](https://www.economist.com/finance-and-economics/2026/09/16/soaring-bond-yields-gaping-deficits-and-towering-debts-what-could-go-wrong).}

### The exorbitant privilege

In an example in LN11b, we concluded that the U.S. could continue running a CA deficit of 4 percent annually, but that it then had to run a trade surplus to stabilize the NIIP relative to GDP.

    We assumed that $i=5%$, both on U.S. assets and liabilities.

    U.S. foreign assets have often earned a higher average return ($r^A$ than the
    return paid on U.S. foreign liabilities ($r^L$).

     A favorable return differential makes it possible to sustain a larger trade
    deficit for a given path of the NIIP-to-GDP ratio.

### The ``exorbitant privilege'' has several dimensions

Dollar dominance may benefit the United States through:
- **lower financing costs**: strong demand for Treasury securities
              and other dollar assets
- **return differential**: relatively high returns on risky foreign
              assets and low returns paid on safe liabilities
- **valuation insurance**: dollar depreciation can improve the
              dollar value of the U.S. external position
- **policy capacity**: dollar liquidity facilities and financial
              sanctions have global reach

    These benefits coexist with possible costs from a stronger currency and a
    larger exposure to shifts in global portfolios.

### A possible cost: a stronger dollar

[-Miran, p. 11]
        "America's status as reserve currency confers the burden of an overvalued currency eroding the competitiveness of our export sector"

## Tariffs, exchange rates and external adjustment

### 5. Do tariffs help improve the trade balance?

[-Miran, p. 13]
        "The critical question is to what extent currencies adjust to offset changes in international tax regimes."

### Miran's diagnosis

Miran's argument can be summarized as a chain:

$$\begin{aligned}
    \text{demand for dollar assets}
        & \Longrightarrow  strong dollar\\
        & \Longrightarrow  \text{trade deficit and pressure on manufacturing.}
\end{aligned}$$

    This is a causal hypothesis, not an identity. Each arrow must be assessed:
- How large is the effect of reserve demand on the dollar?
- How strongly does the trade balance respond to the exchange rate?
- Which other forces determine saving, investment and manufacturing?

### Can a tariff improve the trade balance?

Suppose the United States imposes a tariff to reduce imports.

    The national-income identity still requires

$$NX=S-I.$$
Macroeconomic discipline
        A persistent improvement in net exports must be matched by higher national
        saving, lower domestic investment or both.

    A tariff can change the composition of trade even if aggregate $NX$ changes
    little.

### Adjustment when saving and investment do not change

If a tariff initially reduces import demand but leaves $S-I$ unchanged:
1. demand for foreign currency falls
2. the dollar tends to appreciate
3. the appreciation makes imports cheaper before the tariff and makes
              U.S. exports more expensive abroad
4. imports recover partly and exports decline

    The exchange-rate response therefore offsets part of the tariff's direct
    effect on net exports.

### How can tariffs change $S-I$?

Tariffs may still affect the trade balance through their broader effects:
- tariff revenue may raise public saving – unless it finances other tax
              cuts or spending
- higher prices may alter private saving
- uncertainty and higher input costs may reduce investment
- retaliation may reduce exports and expected income
- changes in expected returns may redirect international capital flows

    An improvement caused by lower investment is quite different from one caused
    by higher saving and productivity.

### What happened after the 2025 tariff shift?

By 2025, the United States had raised tariffs substantially. The early
    macroeconomic evidence does not show rapid external rebalancing:
- The U.S. current-account deficit was 3.7% of GDP in 2025
- The IMF projects a deficit near $3.5$–$4%$ of GDP through 2031
- Tariff-driven goods inflation offset some moderation in services
              inflation during 2025

 See: [IMF 2026 U.S. Article IV](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/04/01/pr-26102-usa-imf-executive-board-concludes-2026-article-iv-consult).

### What does this evidence establish?

- It is consistent with the prediction that tariffs alone do not ensure
              a smaller aggregate trade deficit
- It does *not* imply that tariffs have no effects: relative prices,
              bilateral trade, sectoral production and tariff revenue can all change
- The aggregate current account also reflects fiscal policy, private
              saving, investment and foreign demand for U.S. assets
The useful conclusion
        Trade policy must be analyzed jointly with fiscal policy, capital flows and
        the exchange rate.

### International cooperation: a Mar-a-Lago Accord

[-Miran, p. 28]
        "Historically, multilateral currency accords have been the principal means of implementing international changes in the value of the dollar."

### Could a coordinated depreciation work?

Miran discusses an international agreement intended to weaken the dollar,
    inspired by the 1985 Plaza Accord.

    Possible instruments include:
- coordinated announcements and foreign-exchange intervention
- monetary-policy cooperation
- changes in the maturity or return on official Treasury holdings
- security or trade concessions used to obtain cooperation

    The macroeconomic question is whether these measures alter expected returns,
    saving and investment sufficiently to produce a lasting real depreciation.
See [Obstfeld (2026)](https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/trying-prop-yen-us-wants-have-its-cake-and-eat-it-too) for comments on the recent joint U.S.–Japan fx interventions.

### The Plaza Accord: a useful but imperfect precedent

- The dollar had appreciated strongly in the first half of the 1980s
- In September 1985, the G5 announced that further appreciation of
              nondollar currencies was desirable
- The announcement and intervention were followed by a substantial
              dollar depreciation
- The dollar had already begun to weaken before the agreement
- Fiscal and monetary conditions differed across countries and changed
              over time

    The episode shows that coordination can affect expectations. It does not show
    that exchange-rate management can permanently replace adjustment in saving and
    investment.

## Geoeconomics through a macroeconomic lens

### What is geoeconomics?

**Geoeconomics** is the use of economic instruments to pursue strategic or
    security objectives.

    Examples include:
- tariffs and export controls
- financial sanctions and reserve freezes
- industrial subsidies and investment screening
- payment systems, reserve currencies and central-bank swap lines

    The objectives may be geopolitical, but the transmission remains economic.

### Dollar dominance creates geoeconomic power

Because international payments and financial claims are often denominated
    in dollars, the United States can:
- restrict access to dollar-based financial institutions
- impose financial sanctions and freeze reserve assets
- provide dollar liquidity selectively through central-bank swap
              lines

    These powers are partly a consequence of the same network effects that
    sustain dollar dominance.

    Their use may encourage some countries to diversify their reserves and
    payment arrangements, but the lack of close alternatives makes rapid
    displacement of the dollar unlikely.

### Summing up

- The dollar remains the dominant international currency because of
              market depth, safe assets, institutions and network effects
- Reserve demand can affect yields and exchange rates, but it does not
              mechanically require U.S. current-account deficits
- $CA=S-I$ links external balances to saving and investment
- $NIIP$ dynamics depend on current accounts, growth, returns and
              valuation changes
- Tariffs can reshape trade, but persistent aggregate rebalancing
              requires adjustment in saving or investment
